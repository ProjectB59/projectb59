Computing Machinery and Intelligence

A. M. Turing · Mind · 1950
“I propose to consider the question, ‘Can machines think?’”

Why it matters

Turing replaces an unanswerable question with an operational one. Instead of defining thought, he proposes a test: a human interrogator exchanges typed messages with a person and a machine and tries to tell which is which. If the machine cannot be reliably identified, refusing it the word "thinking" is just prejudice about what it is made of.

The nine objections

Most of the paper answers anticipated objections: theological, mathematical (Gödel), consciousness, "a machine can only do what we tell it," and others. The replies still frame debates about AI seventy years on.

The last section

Turing argues that rather than program an adult mind, one should build a "child machine" and educate it. Learning machines, not hand-coded rules, are the route to the goal. That bet is the one the field eventually took.

SOURCE: A. M. Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Mind, vol. LIX, no. 236 (October 1950), pp. 433 to 460.
