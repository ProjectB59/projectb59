A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity

Warren S. McCulloch & Walter Pitts · Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics · 1943
“For any logical expression satisfying certain conditions, one can find a net behaving in the fashion it describes.”

Why it matters

This is the paper that made the neuron a unit of computation. McCulloch, a neurophysiologist, and Pitts, a self-taught logician still in his teens, modelled the nerve cell as a binary threshold device: it either fires or it does not, and it fires when enough of its inputs are active. From that single idealisation they showed that networks of such units can compute any proposition that can be stated in the propositional calculus.

The claim

The nervous system, on this account, is a logic machine. Perception, memory and inference become questions about what a network of all-or-none elements can and cannot represent. The paper draws the line in both directions: some functions need loops (feedback) to compute, and a net without loops is strictly weaker than one with them.

What it started

Von Neumann cited it in the design of the stored-program computer. It gave Rosenblatt's perceptron and every later artificial neural network their basic vocabulary (units, weights, thresholds, layers). The phrase "neural network" traces to here.

SOURCE: Warren S. McCulloch and Walter Pitts, "A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity," Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics, vol. 5 (1943), pp. 115 to 133.
