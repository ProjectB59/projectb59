As We May Think

Vannevar Bush · The Atlantic Monthly · July 1945
“Consider a future device … in which an individual stores all his books, records, and communications, and which is mechanized so that it may be consulted with exceeding speed and flexibility. It is an enlarged intimate supplement to his memory.”

Why it matters

Bush ran American wartime science. Writing as the war ended, he asked what the country's scientific effort should turn to next, and answered: the organisation of human knowledge. The essay describes the memex, a desk that stores a library on microfilm and lets its owner build permanent associative trails between documents, linking any item to any other and following those links later.

The idea underneath

Indexing by hierarchy and alphabet, Bush argued, is not how minds work; minds work by association, snapping from one item to the next along a web of connections. A machine that worked the same way would be a genuine extension of thought rather than a filing cabinet.

What it started

Douglas Engelbart and Ted Nelson both credited this essay directly; it stands behind the mouse, hypertext, and the web. For artificial intelligence it set an enduring framing: the machine as a partner that augments human intellect, not only a system that imitates it.

SOURCE: Vannevar Bush, "As We May Think," The Atlantic Monthly, July 1945, pp. 101 to 108.
