B59-203.001 · WRITING · DIGITAL CASH

Security without Identification: Card Computers to Make Big Brother Obsolete

David Chaum · Communications of the ACM, 28(10) · October 1985
"Computerization is robbing individuals of the ability to monitor and control the ways information about them is used."

Why it matters

This is the fullest statement of the idea the cypherpunks organized around: that the privacy properties of the systems being automated in the 1980s were being fixed by default, invisibly, and that cryptography offered a genuine alternative if adopted before the identity-based version set. Eric Hughes’ 1993 "A Cypherpunk’s Manifesto" (B59-302.004) is a compression of this argument.

What it proposes

Individuals interact with organizations under different unlinkable pseudonyms, transfer credentials between those pseudonyms without making them linkable, and pay with the untraceable cash of the blind-signature scheme (B59-201.001). Organizations still get authenticated transactions and fraud control; they just do not get a cross-linkable dossier.

Reading it now

The "card computer" is a smartphone. The "mass surveillance from everyday consumer transactions" is the ad-tech and data-broker economy. Chaum described both, and an alternative, in 1985. Which parts were technically necessary and which were choices is the live question the paper still forces.

From the opening (verbatim)

Computerization is robbing individuals of the ability to monitor and control the ways information about them is used. ... New and more serious dangers derive from computerized pattern recognition techniques: even a small group using these and tapping into data gathered in everyday consumer transactions could secretly conduct mass surveillance, inferring individuals’ lifestyles, activities, and associations. ... This conflict may be avoided by early adoption of a fundamentally different approach to automating transaction systems.
SOURCE: David Chaum, "Security without Identification: Transaction Systems to Make Big Brother Obsolete," Communications of the ACM, vol. 28, no. 10, October 1985, pp. 1030-1044.
