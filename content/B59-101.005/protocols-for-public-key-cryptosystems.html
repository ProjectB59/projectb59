B59-101.005 · WRITING · PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTOGRAPHY

Protocols for Public Key Cryptosystems

Ralph C. Merkle · IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy · April 1980
"New cryptographic protocols which take full advantage of the unique properties of public key cryptosystems are now evolving."

Why it matters

This is where the hash tree enters the literature. The problem Merkle solves is authenticating one item out of many without signing or storing each one: publish a single root hash, and prove membership with a logarithmic path of hashes. That data structure turned out to matter far beyond cryptographic protocols.

What it compares

The paper sets the conventional shared-key approach against public key methods for two tasks, distributing keys and signing documents, and weighs the costs of each. It also develops Merkle’s tree-based one-time signature ideas, which he expands in later work.

The lineage it started

Merkle trees authenticate the transaction set in every Bitcoin block and the state in most later chains. Certificate Transparency logs, Git commit history, ZFS, BitTorrent and content-addressed storage systems all rest on the same root-and-path idea introduced here.

Original abstract

New cryptographic protocols which take full advantage of the unique properties of public key cryptosystems are now evolving. Several protocols for public key distribution and for digital signatures are briefly compared with each other and with the conventional alternative.
SOURCE: Ralph C. Merkle, "Protocols for Public Key Cryptosystems," Proceedings of the 1980 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy, Oakland, CA, April 1980, pp. 122-134.
