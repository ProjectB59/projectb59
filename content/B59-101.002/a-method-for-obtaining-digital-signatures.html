B59-101.002 · WRITING · RSA

A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-Key Cryptosystems

R. L. Rivest, A. Shamir, L. Adleman · Communications of the ACM, 21(2) · February 1978
"Publicly revealing an encryption key does not thereby reveal the corresponding decryption key."

Why it matters

Diffie and Hellman described what a public-key system would need to be; RSA is the first one that fully delivered it. Encryption and signatures from a single operation, security reducible to a problem (factoring) that had been studied for centuries, and simple enough to implement on the hardware of the day.

How it works

Pick two large primes, publish their product n and an exponent e, keep the primes secret. Encrypting is raising a message to the power e mod n; decrypting is raising to a secret power d recovered from the primes. Signing runs the same math in the other order. Recovering d without the primes appears to require factoring n.

The lineage it started

RSA underlies decades of TLS, PGP and GPG, SSH, code signing, and smart-card identity. The cypherpunks built on it directly: PGP is an RSA application, and Chaum’s digital cash uses RSA signatures with a blinding step (see B59-201.001).

Original abstract

An encryption method is presented with the novel property that publicly revealing an encryption key does not thereby reveal the corresponding decryption key. This has two important consequences: (1) Couriers or other secure means are not needed to transmit keys... (2) A message can be "signed" using a privately held decryption key. Anyone can verify this signature using the corresponding publicly revealed encryption key. Signatures cannot be forged, and a signer cannot later deny the validity of his signature. This has obvious applications in "electronic mail" and "electronic funds transfer" systems.
