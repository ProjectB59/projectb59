B59-303.001 · WRITING · MIX NETWORKS

Untraceable Electronic Mail, Return Addresses, and Digital Pseudonyms

David L. Chaum · Communications of the ACM, 24(2) · February 1981
"A technique... that allows an electronic mail system to hide who a participant communicates with as well as the content of the communication."

Why it matters

Encryption hides message content; it does nothing about the pattern of who contacts whom. Chaum was the first to treat that metadata as the harder problem and to give a construction that beats it without a trusted party. The cypherpunk remailer culture is a direct application of this paper.

The mechanism

Wrap a message in nested public-key encryption, one layer per mix. Each mix strips its layer, holds messages until it has a batch, shuffles them, and forwards. An observer sees a message go into the first mix and a different-looking message come out, with no way to match them. Return addresses work the same way in reverse. Anonymity holds as long as at least one mix in the path is honest.

The lineage it started

Type I (cypherpunk) and Type II (Mixmaster) remailers, Mixminion, onion routing, and Tor are all mix-network designs. Digital pseudonyms, the idea of a persistent unlinkable identity you control, run from here through Chaum’s credential work (B59-203.001) into modern anonymous-credential systems.

Original abstract

A technique based on public key cryptography is presented that allows an electronic mail system to hide who a participant communicates with as well as the content of the communication -- in spite of an unsecured underlying telecommunication system. The technique does not require a universally trusted authority. One correspondent can remain anonymous to a second, while allowing the second to respond via an untraceable return address. The technique can also be used to form rosters of untraceable digital pseudonyms from selected applications.
SOURCE: David L. Chaum, "Untraceable Electronic Mail, Return Addresses, and Digital Pseudonyms," Communications of the ACM, vol. 24, no. 2, February 1981, pp. 84-88.
